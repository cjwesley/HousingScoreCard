Homebuying Income Gap, With and Without Property Taxes

MoneyLion’s 2026 Mortgage Stress Index computes each state’s homebuying income gap from principal-and-interest mortgage costs only, excluding property taxes. This table takes the five largest published deficits and the five largest published surpluses and adds each state’s effective property tax on its typical single-family home. Click any column header to sort. Illinois is highlighted. With taxes included, Illinois loses its #1 surplus ranking to West Virginia and Ohio’s surplus flips negative.

Sources. Median household income and published gaps: MoneyLion, 2026 Mortgage Stress Index (July 21, 2026); their income-needed figures assume a 10% down payment, the 6.47% national average 30-year fixed rate as of May 23, 2026, and the 50/30/20 rule with essentials capped at 50% of income. MoneyLion’s own published payment examples (Maryland $2,461, Massachusetts $3,784) reproduce exactly as pure principal and interest at those terms (a third worked example, Hawaii, is omitted because its cited home value could not be re-verified against the archived study text), confirming the mortgage-payment component excludes property taxes and insurance. Home values: Zillow ZHVI, single-family mid-tier, seasonally adjusted, June 2026, matching MoneyLion’s data-as-of date of June 23, 2026. Effective property tax rates: U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2023 5-year, median real estate taxes paid (B25103) divided by median owner-occupied home value (B25077), per state; these reflect current owners after exemptions and assessment lag; where those mechanisms are significant a new buyer faces more, a wedge quantified in the companion analysis only for Oak Park, and the with-tax gaps are conservative to that extent. Gap + tax = published gap − 2 × (effective rate × SFR value); the doubling follows from MoneyLion’s 50% essentials cap, under which each dollar of added essential cost raises income needed by two dollars. Green marks the best value in a column, red the worst. Table covers only the five largest published deficits and five largest published surpluses of the 50 states, per scope; it is not a full national re-ranking.
